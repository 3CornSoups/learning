Beyond Retrieval: Structuring Evolving and Inconsistent External
Knowledge with Proposition Relations for RAG
Noriki Nishida1 Fei Cheng2,3 Yuji Matsumoto4,5
1National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology (AIST)
2Kyoto University 3NII LLMC 4Tohoku University 5RIKEN

Abstract
Real-world knowledge bases evolve over time
and accumulate outdated statements, contradic-
tions, and misinformation. LLMs have been
reported to be vulnerable to such inconsistent
contexts. In this paper, we propose Proposition-
Structured RAG (ProStruct-RAG), a frame-
work that continuously structures evolving and
inconsistent external knowledge bases using
timestamped propositions and inter-proposition
relations. ProStruct-RAG represents a docu-
ment collection as a graph whose nodes are
propositions and whose edges capture tempo-
ral updates, contradictions, and support rela-
tions, retrieves query-relevant knowledge as
a relational structure, and uses it for answer
generation in RAG. Experiments on multiple
open-book temporal QA benchmarks show that
ProStruct-RAG outperforms baselines that do
not use inter-proposition relations, suggesting
that RAG over evolving and inconsistent knowl-
edge benefits from organizing external knowl-
edge before retrieval, rather than relying solely
on inference-time reasoning. We release our
code and Asteria, a new fictional temporal QA
dataset designed to mitigate data contamina-
tion.1
1 Introduction
The internal knowledge of Large Language Models
(LLMs) inevitably becomes outdated. To address
this problem, continual fine-tuning (Dhingra et al.,
2022; Jang et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2025) and
localized parameter editing (De Cao et al., 2021;
Mitchell et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2025b; Shi et al.,
2025; Wang et al., 2025) have been proposed to
update the internal knowledge. However, because
these methods repeatedly update model parameters,
they are prone to catastrophic forgetting and make
it difficult to maintain long-term knowledge con-
sistency (Onoe et al., 2023; Gekhman et al., 2024;
Niu et al., 2025). Retrieval-Augmented Generation
1https://github.com/norikinishida/kapipe
(RAG), which retrieves an external knowledge base
at inference time to generate evidence-grounded
answers, has therefore been widely adopted (Lewis
et al., 2020; Izacard et al., 2023; Laitenberger et al.,
2025).
However, external knowledge bases are not al-
ways static and consistent. They continuously
evolve and are often accumulated in an unorga-
nized form, containing temporally outdated state-
ments, mutually conflicting claims, and unreliable
content. Recent efforts on long-context modeling,
reasoning-enhanced LLMs, and agentic systems
aim to let LLMs integrate information and resolve
contradictions from such inconsistent contexts at
inference time (Li et al., 2024; Du et al., 2025; An
et al., 2025). However, LLMs have been reported
to be vulnerable to contexts that mix temporal up-
dates, contradictions, and misinformation (Ouyang
et al., 2025; Hou et al., 2024).
To address this challenge, prior work has or-
ganized external knowledge before retrieval. For
example, Ge et al. (2025) showed that tempo-
rally organizing multi-session dialogues as time-
lines improves memory retrieval and reasoning. Li
et al. (2025) decomposed documents intopropo-
sitions(Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025) and
dynamically labeled their truthfulness. However,
these methods organize knowledge as collections
of facts, without explicitly modeling rich relations
between them. Although other work structures
knowledge through entity relations (Edge et al.,
2024; Han et al., 2025; Nishida et al., 2026), tem-
poral updates, contradictions, and support arise
between individual facts rather than entities. This
distinction is important because temporal validity
and misinformation are not properties of isolated
propositions alone.
In this paper, we aim to continually organize
evolving and inconsistent external knowledge bases
through relations between propositions. Our goal
is to examine whether part of the burden of in-

tegrating such knowledge can be shifted from
inference-time reasoning to pre-retrieval knowl-
edge organization. Our core hypothesis is that
explicit inter-proposition relations help LLMs inte-
grate evidence and resolve temporal updates, con-
tradictions, and misinformation. Specifically, we
propose Proposition-Structured RAG (ProStruct-
RAG), which represents external knowledge as a
set of timestamped propositions and structures it
through inter-proposition relations. First, we ex-
tract concise propositions from a document col-
lection and assign each proposition a timestamp
based on the publication date of its source docu-
ment. Next, we identify updates, contradicts,
and supports relations between propositions and
construct a proposition graph. At inference time,
we retrieve a query-specific subgraph relevant to
the user question. Finally, we convert the retrieved
query-specific subgraph into a structured context,
and the QA model generates the final answer.
We evaluate ProStruct-RAG on open-book tem-
poral QA benchmarks, where answers can change
with the target time and models must interpret re-
trieved knowledge under temporal updates and con-
tradictions. Our results show that proposition de-
composition alone does not consistently improve
performance, while adding temporal information
yields substantial gains. Making temporal update,
contradiction, and support relations explicit fur-
ther improves overall performance, surpassing all
baselines on average. These results indicate that
RAG over evolving, inconsistent knowledge ben-
efits from organizing external knowledge through
inter-proposition relations before retrieval, rather
than leaving integration and contradiction resolu-
tion entirely to inference-time reasoning.
2 Related Work
Long-Context RAG for Knowledge Obsoles-
cence.LLMs store factual knowledge internally,
but it inevitably becomes outdated, degrading per-
formance on time-sensitive tasks (Hu et al., 2025;
Song et al., 2025; Gekhman et al., 2025; Luu et al.,
2022). Continual fine-tuning and parameter edit-
ing update such knowledge (Dhingra et al., 2022;
Jang et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2025; De Cao et al.,
2021; Mitchell et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2025b; Sun
et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), but
suffer from catastrophic forgetting and poor tem-
poral or relational consistency (Onoe et al., 2023;
Gekhman et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2025). Retrieval-
Augmented Generation (RAG) addresses outdated
or incomplete internal knowledge by consulting
external sources (Lewis et al., 2020; Izacard et al.,
2023; Laitenberger et al., 2025), but errors often
stem from missing or noisy evidence (Yang et al.,
2024; Leto et al., 2024). While long-context mod-
els can outperform standard RAG (Li et al., 2024),
simply increasing context length can even degrade
reasoning performance (Goldman et al., 2024; Bai
et al., 2025; Gupta et al., 2025; Du et al., 2025; An
et al., 2025). These findings motivate approaches
that structure long-context information rather than
relying on unstructured context expansion.
Knowledge Organization for RAG.Prior work
shows that RAG systems and LLMs are vulnerable
to temporally evolving facts, conflicting evidence,
and false information (Hou et al., 2024; Ouyang
et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2023). To address temporal
change, several benchmarks and methods organize
knowledge along timelines (Kasai et al., 2023; Zhu
et al., 2025), while others maintain truthfulness
labels for each fact (Li et al., 2025) or dynamic
representations such as temporal knowledge graphs
and time-aware memories (Lu et al., 2024; Ge et al.,
2025). However, these phenomena are typically
studied in isolation, despite frequently co-occurring
in practice. In contrast, this work enables unified
evaluation and modeling of their interactions within
a single framework.
Proposition-based RAG and Context Enrich-
ment.Related work explores the appropriate unit
of knowledge and retrieval granularity, including
proposition-level retrieval (Chen et al., 2024), struc-
tural expansion of retrieved units (Tao et al., 2025),
and fact extraction pipelines tailored for verifica-
tion (Liu et al., 2025). Other studies revisit retrieval
objectives, arguing that semantic similarity alone
is insufficient and proposing retrievers optimized
for downstream utility (Xu et al., 2025c,a). Sev-
eral approaches also enrich prompts with supple-
mentary information, such as timestamps (Kasai
et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2025) or hypothetical re-
trieval scenarios (Lee et al., 2025). While these
methods improve retrieval granularity, selection
criteria, or contextual richness, they largely treat
retrieved knowledge as independent units. Combin-
ing proposition-level retrieval (Chen et al., 2024)
with timestamp information (Kasai et al., 2023;
Zhu et al., 2025) follows this design: time informa-
tion is attached to individual propositions, while
their temporal and logical relationships remain im-

3 Methods
ProStruct-RAG aims to continually organize an
evolving and inconsistent external knowledge base
into a graph of timestamped propositions and inter-
proposition relations. At inference time, it retrieves
and presents relevant knowledge as a relational
structure, enabling LLMs to better integrate infor-
mation and resolve temporal updates, contradic-
tions, and misinformation.
Figure 1 shows the framework overview.
ProStruct-RAG has four steps:Proposition Ex-
tractiondecomposes documents into concise, self-
contained propositions with temporal information;
Inter-Proposition Relation Extractionidentifies
updates, contradictions, and support relations to
build a proposition graph;Subgraph Retrievalre-
trieves question-relevant anchor propositions and
expands them to a query-specific subgraph with
neighboring propositions and relations; andAn-
swer Generationlinearizes the subgraph into struc-
tured textual context for the QA model. The first
two steps are performed offline, while retrieval and
generation are performed at inference time.
3.1 Proposition Extraction
Definition of propositions.Apropositionis a
concise, self-contained mention of a fact, used
as the minimal knowledge unit in ProStruct-RAG.
Context-dependent expressions such as pronouns
are replaced with their corresponding entities so
that each proposition can be interpreted indepen-
dently. Each proposition is assigned a timestamp in
the YYYY-MM-DD format, using the publication date
of its source document.2 This timestamp is used in
downstream steps.
2An alternative is to use the time when the described fact
became true, or the period during which it remained true.
While more direct, this information is often unavailable in
real-world documents and difficult to define for temporally
invariant facts. We therefore use source publication dates,
which can be obtained stably from document collections.

Extraction procedure.We use an LLM to ex-
tract propositions from the document collection.
For each document, the LLM generates multiple
concise, self-contained propositions in a single
pass, guided by a small set of manually annotated
examples. Each proposition inherits the timestamp
of its source document. We apply this process
to all documents and aggregate the outputs into
theproposition collectionused in subsequent steps.
The prompt template used for proposition extrac-
tion is provided in Appendix A.
3.2 Inter-Proposition Relation Extraction
Definition of inter-proposition relations.Exist-
ing methods often assign factuality labels to propo-
sitions independently. In contrast, we represent
factuality through inter-proposition relations, mod-
eling whether a proposition updates, contradicts,
or supports another proposition. We define inter-
proposition relations as directed: the subject (head)
is a temporallynewerproposition, and the object
(tail) is from thesameor anearliertime. This
constraint captures how newer information updates,
contradicts, or supports existing knowledge. Ta-
ble 1 shows the four relation classes.
Extraction procedure.Classifying all proposi-
tion pairs is inefficient, so we first retrieve candi-
date pairs likely to have relations. For each propo-
sition, treated as the subject, we retrieve the top
20 semantically related propositions with the same
or earlier timestamps as object candidates using
an off-the-shelf dense retriever.3 This limits subse-
quent classification to likely relation pairs.
Then, for each candidate pair, an LLM classifies
the relation as one of the four classes in Table 1:
updates, contradicts, supports, or NOREL. For
efficiency, candidate pairs sharing the same subject
are grouped into a single prompt and classified in
batch. As in proposition extraction, we use few-
shot examples covering all relation classes.
To improve relation quality, we further refine
pairs initially labeled as relations, i.e., labels other
than NOREL. A stronger LLM re-evaluates each pair,
removes pairs relabeled as NOREL, and updates rela-
tion labels when necessary. As before, we provide
few-shot examples and explicit decision criteria for
each label. The prompt templates used for inter-
proposition relation classification and refinement
are provided in Appendix A.
Proposition graph construction.We construct
a proposition graph G= (V,E) , where each node
v∈ V is a proposition and each directed edge
e∈ E is an inter-proposition relation from a subject
to an object proposition.
3.3 Subgraph Retrieval
At inference time, given a question, we retrieve
relevant knowledge from the proposition graph as a
relational structure. The goal is to retrieve not only
question-relevant propositions, but also their rela-
tions to other propositions, such as updates, con-
tradictions, and support. This provides the LLM
with structured external knowledge rather than an
unorganized set of propositions. Specifically, sub-
graph retrieval consists of two stages: (1) dense
retrieval of question-relevant anchor propositions,
followed by (2) one-hop expansion over the propo-
sition graph.
Specifically, we first retrieve the top-k proposi-
tion nodes relevant to question q using an off-the-
shelf dense retriever, and denote them as anchor
nodes Aq. These anchors serve as starting points
for subgraph expansion. Here, k denotes the num-
ber of anchor propositions retrieved before expan-

3.4 Answer Generation
Structured context construction.Since a stan-
dard QA model cannot directly consume the
retrieved query-specific subgraph Gq, we con-
vert it into a structured textual context Cq =
Contextualize(Vq,E q). This context verbalizes
both proposition content and inter-proposition re-
lations in a QA-model-readable form. Specifically,
the instance-specific temporal and structural infor-
mation is contained in Cq: each proposition in-
cludes its timestamp, statement, and incoming and
outgoing relations to other retrieved propositions.
The response-generation instructions are shared
across questions and specify how the QA model
should use these temporal and relational cues in the
retrieved context.
Globally, we sort proposition nodes in chrono-
logical order to show how external knowledge
changed over time. Locally, we verbalize each
proposition as a textual item, as illustrated in Fig-
ure 2. Each item contains the proposition ID, times-
tamp, statement, and its inter-proposition relations.
Proposition IDs are assigned after chronological
sorting, enabling consistent references within the
structured context. Relations are verbalized in nat-
ural language rather than as mechanical edge la-
bels. For example, an updates relation specifies
both its direction, i.e., whether the current propo-
sition updates or is updated by another proposi-
tion, and the counterpart proposition. We verbalize
contradicts and supports relations in the same
direction-aware manner. This lets the QA model
interpret each proposition within its temporal and
logical context, rather than as an isolated sentence.4

Answer generation.Finally, we input the ques-
tion q, structured context Cq, and generation in-
structions into the QA model to produce the an-
swer: a= QA(q,C q). We prompt the model to
generate a rationale before the final answer, encour-
aging it to explicitly use the retrieved propositions
and inter-proposition relations.
4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setup
Datasets.We evaluate our method on open-book
temporal QA benchmarks. Each dataset consists
of a temporally annotated knowledge corpus and
QA pairs. To make the target time explicit, each
question is given to the QA model as “{question}
(Date: {timestamp}) ”. These benchmarks re-
quire models to answer questions whose correct
answers can change with the target time; thus, mod-
els must not only retrieve relevant knowledge, but
also interpret how it evolves over time. Specifi-
cally, we use the following datasets: (1)CLARK-
News(Li et al., 2025) is a temporal QA bench-
mark constructed from real web articles. We use
only QA pairs whose answer changes at least three
times for the same question text, and remove cases
with multiple correct answers at the same time
point. (2)StreamingQA(Liška et al., 2022) is
a temporal open-domain QA benchmark in which
questions are grounded in dates and answered us-
ing timestamped news articles spanning 14 years.
We randomly sample 200 QA pairs from its test
split. (3)Asteriais an open-book temporal QA
dataset based on a fictional world, which we con-
from that of additional graph-native search or reasoning mech-
anisms.

4.3 Ablation Study
To analyze component contributions, we conduct
ablations on inter-proposition relations. All exper-
iments use Asteria with retrieval size k= 400 ,
GPT-4o as the QA model, and the same settings
as the main experiments. We remove components
from the full method one at a time, covering re-
lation construction, relation labels, and individual
relation types. The results are shown in Table 4.
Is relation refinement necessary?We first com-
pare the full method with a variant without relation
refinement, testing whether initially extracted rela-
tions are sufficient for downstream QA. Removing
refinement clearly reduces performance, showing
that relation quality, not merely relation quantity,
is important. Because our method passes retrieved

propositions to the QA model as a relational struc-
ture, incorrect relations become inference-time
noise. Thus, relation refinement is a central com-
ponent of our method. This result also indicates
that the proposed framework is sensitive to relation
quality.
Is the meaning of relation labels necessary?
Next, we replace all edges with a single generic
relation while preserving the graph structure, test-
ing whether gains come from connectivity alone or
from relation-label semantics. Removing relation
labels clearly reduces performance, showing that
graph structure alone is insufficient. In temporal
QA, knowing that two propositions are connected
is not enough; the model must know whether one
updates, contradicts, or supports the other. Thus,
relation labels are essential cues for interpreting
the retrieved proposition set.
Do temporal update, contradiction, and support
relations contribute complementarily?We fur-
ther remove one relation type at a time: updates,
contradicts, or supports. This tests whether
gains depend on a single relation type or on their
complementarity. Performance drops in all cases,
indicating that each relation type plays a distinct
role. The updates relation captures how new infor-
mation overwrites old information, contradicts
exposes conflicting claims, and supports links
claims to reinforcing evidence. Thus, our method
benefits from combining multiple relations to rep-
resent both changes and consistency in knowledge.
4.4 Diagnostic Analysis
We conduct a diagnostic analysis of how fake
propositions are handled in the proposition graph.
Since Asteria annotates each proposition as fake
or non-fake at construction time, we analyze how
fake propositions connect to others through inter-
proposition relations. We ask: (1) whether fake
propositions are isolated, and (2) which relation
types connect them to other propositions.
Are fake propositions isolated in the graph?To
determine whether fake propositions are explicitly
corrected in the graph, we count incomingupdates
and contradicts relations, which can revise or in-
validate a proposition. We exclude supports from
this measure because it does not correct or invali-
date the supported proposition. The results show
that 57.6% of fake propositions are connected in
this way, suggesting that most fake propositions
are not isolated noise. Instead, many are incor-
porated into contradiction relations or temporal
update chains, allowing inter-proposition relations
to position fake propositions relative to other evi-
dence.
Which relation types connect fake propositions?
We compare incoming relation labels for fake and
regular propositions, focusing on how temporally
newer propositions refer to them. Figure 3 shows
that fake propositions are most often connected by
contradicts relations, whereas non-fake (regular)
propositions are most often connected by updates
relations; supports relations are rare in both cases.
This suggests that fake propositions tend to be de-
nied by newer propositions, while regular propo-
sitions tend to be updated as part of factual tran-
sitions. Thus, our relations partially distinguish
fake information from ordinary temporal updates,
although the few supports links to fake proposi-
tions suggest residual classification errors.
5 Conclusion
We proposed ProStruct-RAG, a framework that
structures evolving and inconsistent external knowl-
edge before retrieval through inter-proposition re-
lations. Across multiple open-book temporal QA
datasets, ProStruct-RAG improved over baselines
that do not use inter-propositional relations in most
evaluation settings. These results suggest that,
when RAG operates over evolving and inconsistent
knowledge, organizing external knowledge rela-
tionally before retrieval can complement inference-
time reasoning for evidence integration and contra-
diction resolution.
Limitations
This work has several limitations.
First, our evaluation is conducted on a limited set
of datasets that are closely aligned with news-style
6757

===== PDF PAGE 10 =====
content, including both real-world news articles
and a controlled dataset designed to mimic news-
like temporal dynamics. While such domains natu-
rally involve temporal updates, contradictions, and
unreliable mentions, the need for external knowl-
edge grounding is often more critical in more spe-
cialized or industry-specific domains, such as sci-
entific literature or enterprise internal documents.
Our current experiments do not assess the effective-
ness of the proposed framework in such specialized
domains, and its generalizability to more special-
ized settings remains an open question.
Second, our QA experiments are limited to three
models: GPT-4o, o3, and Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct.
These models were selected to cover a general-
purpose proprietary model, a reasoning-oriented
model, and an open-weight model, and the ob-
served effects of temporal and relational knowl-
edge organization are broadly consistent across
them. Nevertheless, this evaluation does not es-
tablish generalization across the wider range of
LLM families, model scales, and context-handling
capabilities. A more extensive comparison with
additional proprietary and open-weight models is
needed to assess the robustness of the findings more
comprehensively.
Third, the methods used for proposition extrac-
tion, inter-proposition relation extraction, and sub-
graph retrieval are not exhaustively studied. We
rely on standard design choices for these compo-
nents, but alternative approaches could affect the
quality of the proposition graph and, consequently,
downstream performance. We do not directly quan-
tify the intrinsic accuracy or completeness of the
extracted propositions. More fundamentally, there
is currently no established consensus on the optimal
decomposition, granularity, or content of proposi-
tions used as external knowledge for LLMs. Deter-
mining which proposition representations are most
effective as external knowledge and systematically
comparing alternative extraction and retrieval meth-
ods remain important directions for future work.
Finally, our framework linearizes the retrieved
proposition subgraph into a structured textual con-
text rather than performing graph-native reasoning
directly over the graph. We also do not compare
alternative linearizations of the same proposition
subgraph, and determining which representation
is most effective for LLM reasoning remains an
open question. How to search and traverse a graph
defined by updates, contradicts, and supports
is an important research question and may further
improve the use of structured knowledge. Graph-
based exploration methods, such as Graph Chain-
of-Thought (Jin et al., 2024), could provide a basis
for such reasoning. However, designing and eval-
uating graph-native traversal and reasoning strate-
gies constitutes a separate research problem. We
therefore focus on whether explicitly structuring
evolving knowledge with inter-proposition rela-
tions is itself beneficial, and leave graph-native
reasoning for future work.

B Asteria Dataset
B.1 Dataset Construction
Asteria was constructed in three stages.
First, we used Gemini 3 Flash (Google Deep-
Mind, 2025) to design a fully fictional world in or-
der to reduce reliance on the internal knowledge of
the evaluated LLMs. We defined 20 major events,
including an energy crisis and resource transition,
the outbreak of a novel disease and discovery of its
treatment, and corruption in political organizations.
For each event, we constructed a timeline of times-
tamped propositions containing temporal updates,
contradictions, and misinformation.

Second, we generated temporal questions
grounded in this external knowledge base. Each
question was designed so that its correct answer
changes over time, and the same question string
can therefore have different answers depending on
the query date.
Third, the generated propositions and QA pairs
were verified using Gemini 3 and manual inspec-

options per question. The final dataset contains 180

C.2 Maintenance Cost
After the initial graph is constructed, it is updated
incrementally as new documents are added. For
each new document, propositions are extracted in
a single LLM call. For each extracted proposi-
tion, a fixed number of semantically related ex-
isting propositions are retrieved as candidate ob-
jects. Candidate pairs sharing the same subject
are classified together in a single prompt, and only
those initially classified as non-NORELare subse-
quently refined. Existing documents and relations
do not need to be reprocessed. As shown in Ta-
ble 8, approximately 11–19% of candidate pairs
proceed to refinement, and approximately 10–14%
are ultimately retained as graph edges. Assuming a
broadly similar number of extracted propositions
per document, the maintenance cost therefore de-
pends primarily on the number and arrival rate of
new documents. Storage grows with the number of
proposition nodes and retained relation edges.
The total operational cost depends on the docu-
ment arrival rate, document length, model or API
pricing, storage implementation, retrieval size, and
query volume.
This design is advantageous when the main-
tained graph is reused to answer many questions.
In contrast, if documents are added frequently but
only a small number of questions are asked, the
preprocessing cost may not be justified.

C.4 Comparison with Standard RAG
Compared with standard chunk-based RAG,
ProStruct-RAG requires additional indexing com-
putation and storage for proposition extraction, re-
lation classification, and graph maintenance. At
inference time, however, the stored relations are
reused across queries: relation classification is
not repeated, and the additional operations consist
mainly of neighborhood lookup and deterministic
context construction. Relation-based expansion
may also increase the amount of context provided
to the QA model.
C.5 Persistent vs. On-the-Fly Graph
Construction
Inference-time factuality methods construct
response-specific graphs from generated claims
and retrieved evidence (Marinescu et al., 2025;
Carnerero-Cano et al., 2026). FactReasoner
uses such a graph to assess factuality, while
FactCorrector uses structured feedback to revise
a generated response. These methods therefore
construct and use the graph after an initial response
has been generated. ProStruct-RAG operates at
a different stage: it constructs a persistent graph
over timestamped propositions before retrieval
and uses the graph for both retrieval and context
construction.
A more general alternative is to retrieve proposi-
tions first and infer relations among them separately
for each query. Table 9 summarizes the concep-

ProStruct-RAG.

D Information Sufficiency of
Propositions.
Propositions extracted by LLMs are designed to be
concise abstractions of source documents. A key
question is whether this abstraction retains suffi-
cient information for accurate question answering.
Because a document does not have a uniquely de-
fined ground-truth proposition decomposition, this
analysis does not directly measure intrinsic extrac-
tion accuracy or completeness. Instead, it evalu-
ates task-level information sufficiency: if the ex-
tracted propositions omit information required for
QA, adding the corresponding source documents
should improve performance. To assess the infor-
mation sufficiency of propositions, we augment
the retrieved proposition subgraph with a small
set of highly relevant source documents. Given
the retrieved subgraph Gq = (V q,E q), we collect
the source documents from which propositions in
V q were derived. Each document is scored by the
number of retrieved propositions it contains, and
the top-r documents are selected as auxiliary ref-
erence documents. These documents are provided
as additional context together with the proposition
subgraph.
As shown in Table 10, adding auxiliary refer-
ence documents does not yield a consistent benefit.
Providing two reference documents slightly im-
proves accuracy, suggesting that source documents
can sometimes complement propositions with de-
tails that are omitted during abstraction. However,
providing only one reference document leads to a
small drop in accuracy. This suggests that auxiliary
documents introduce a trade-off: they may pro-
vide missing details, but they also increase context
length and introduce additional irrelevant or redun-
dant information. When only a single document is
added, the added context may not contain enough
complementary evidence to offset this additional
noise. With two documents, the model may have
access to a broader set of supporting details, which
can occasionally compensate for information omit-
ted from the proposition subgraph. Nevertheless,
the gain is small and not systematic. Overall, these
results suggest that the proposition subgraph al-
ready preserves most of the information necessary
for QA, while auxiliary source documents provide
only limited and unstable additional value.

E Data Contamination Issue in Temporal
QA
Data contamination remains an important con-
cern even in temporal QA. Although temporal QA
benchmarks are designed to evaluate whether mod-
els can use external temporal knowledge, high ac-
curacy may partly reflect parametric knowledge
rather than successful use of retrieved evidence.
Table 3 supports this concern. In the no-context
setting, where no external knowledge is provided,
models achieve far above the random baseline on
StreamingQA: GPT-4o obtains 74.0%, Llama-3.1-
70B-Instruct obtains 65.5%, and o3 obtains 81.5%,
despite the task being four-choice QA. The large
variation across models further suggests that perfor-
mance is affected by each model’s internal knowl-
edge and answer-choice priors.
Asteria is designed to mitigate this issue. It is
built from newly generated fictional events, entities,
and temporal changes, making the required facts
unlikely to be stored in model parameters. Consis-
tent with this design, Table 3 shows that no-context
accuracy on Asteria remains much lower than on
StreamingQA. Asteria therefore provides a cleaner
testbed for evaluating whether a RAG method can
use an evolving external knowledge base, rather
than memorized world knowledge. This property
helps isolate the contribution of ProStruct-RAG’s
explicit temporal and relational structure.
